Electric Vehicle Charging Optimization

Home EV charging is often the largest single electricity load in a household, so when you charge has a big effect on cost and carbon emissions.

How Much Energy an EV Uses:
- Most EVs use 25-35 kWh per 100 miles. A Tesla Model 3 uses about 25 kWh per 100 miles.
- A typical daily commute of 30-40 miles requires 8-12 kWh of charging.
- A Level 2 home charger (240 V) delivers 7-11 kW, adding 25-35 miles of range per hour. A Level 1 outlet (120 V) delivers about 1.4 kW, adding 3-5 miles per hour.

Choosing the Best Time to Charge:
- Avoid charging during peak hours (commonly 4 PM to 9 PM on weekdays), when rates can be 2-3 times the off-peak rate.
- If you have solar and the car is home during the day, charge during the solar window (about 10 AM to 3 PM on sunny days). Solar charging is effectively free and zero-emission.
- If the car is away during the day or the forecast is cloudy, charge during off-peak hours (typically 10 PM to 6 AM).
- Use the car's or charger's scheduled charging feature so charging finishes shortly before you leave, which also keeps the battery warm in cold weather.

Battery Health:
- For daily driving, set the charge limit to 80-90%. Charge to 100% only before long trips.
- Avoid leaving the battery near 0% or 100% for long periods.
- Frequent DC fast charging is more expensive and adds battery wear; home Level 2 charging is the best default.

Cost Example:
- Charging 10 kWh at a peak rate of $0.27/kWh costs $2.70. The same charge at an off-peak rate of $0.09/kWh costs $0.90, saving $1.80 per charge, or about $650 per year for daily charging.
- Charging the same 10 kWh entirely from rooftop solar avoids the grid cost entirely and about 3.9 kg of CO2 per charge.

Smart Charging Features:
- Many chargers support solar-following mode, which adjusts the charging rate to match surplus solar production.
- Utility EV rate plans and managed charging programs can offer even lower overnight rates or bill credits.
